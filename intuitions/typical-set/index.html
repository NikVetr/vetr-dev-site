Where is the probability mass?

A standard normal in k dimensions has its highest density at the mode, its centre. Yet a draw from it almost never lands near the centre. The density falls with the distance r from the mode, but the volume at distance r grows like rk−1, and the mass, density × volume, piles up in a thin shell at r ≈ √k. The panels below show that shell, the original script's version of the argument, two pictures where a mode or a mean misleads in one dimension, and why the points of a sphere crowd its equator.

1 Density × volume

The share of the mass at distance r from the mode is the density there, (2π)−k/2 e−r²/2, times the area of the sphere of radius r, which grows like rk−1. Their product is the chi distribution with k degrees of freedom. It peaks at √(k − 1), not at 0, and its spread stays near 1/√2 however large k gets: the mass lives in a shell of fixed thickness that moves outward.

2 The cube criterion, by family

The original script made the argument with a cube instead of a sphere. Take the cube [−a, a]k around the centre of k independent coordinates, each with density d. Its volume is (2a)k and its smallest density, at a corner, is d(a)k, so their product is (2a d(a))k. That is largest at the same a* in every dimension, so the corner's distance from the centre, a*√k, grows exactly like √k, for every family. Where the mass actually goes is a different question: compare the sampled radii.

3 When the mode misleads

Even in one dimension the highest point of a density need not be where the mass is. Lower a water level from the peak: the density above it is the highest-density region, and what it covers is its mass. A tall narrow spike surfaces first however little it holds, two bumps leave the mean and the median stranded between them, and a region can split in two.

4 A sphere's points crowd its equator

The same geometry from another side: pick a point uniformly on the unit sphere in k dimensions and look at one coordinate. Its density is Γ(k/2) / (√π Γ((k − 1)/2)) (1 − x²)(k−3)/2, so (x₁ + 1)/2 is Beta((k − 1)/2, (k − 1)/2). Its SD is 1/√k: in high dimension almost every point is near the equator of any axis you choose. With three dimensions it is uniform, which is Archimedes' hat-box theorem.

How it works

The shell

For a standard normal in k dimensions the distance from the mode, R, has density 21−k/2 rk−1 e−r²/2 / Γ(k/2): the chi distribution. R² is chi-square with k degrees of freedom, so the share within a ball of radius ρ is P(k/2, ρ²/2), the regularized incomplete gamma function, and every quantile and interval on this page comes from that function and its inverse. The mean is √2 Γ((k + 1)/2) / Γ(k/2), close to √(k − ½), and the variance k minus its square, which tends to ½.

Why a mode is a poor summary

The density at the shell is e−(k−1)/2 times the density at the mode, yet the shell holds nearly all the mass and a ball of radius 1 around the mode almost none. A sampler or an optimiser that reports the mode reports a point that typical draws never come near. The highest-density region of a normal is a ball around the mode, but in high dimension nearly all of that ball's mass, like nearly all of its volume, lies close to its surface.

Families other than the normal

The cube criterion gives √k for every family because it factorises: its answer is the half-width a* that maximises a d(a), times √k. The cube at a* holds only P(|X| ≤ a*)k of the mass, which vanishes as k grows, so the criterion does not locate the mass. What does is the law of large numbers: R²/k tends to the variance, so for any family with a finite variance σ² the sampled radius tracks σ√k. The Cauchy has none, and its radius grows like k, not √k.

Method

Closed forms throughout, except the two samples, which are seeded Monte Carlo and labelled so: points are drawn by inversion from shared/rng.js's mulberry32, and the same seed gives the same points in every browser and in Node. The highest-density regions are found exactly: the density's crossings of the water level by bisection, and their masses from the normal and Laplace distribution functions. Scripts and agents can run the same computations through the API.

The R scripts

The panels come from three R scripts. The cube script centres its intervals one grid step left of 0, on −0.01, and measures the distance to the far end, so each reported distance is one step too long (1.01√k for the normal instead of √k); its comment says its Laplace has variance 1, but dexp(|x|) has variance 2, and this page uses the unit-variance Laplace the comment describes; its mass-contained term adds an interval's width where it means the log of the width (the term is never used). The bathtub animation weighs its first grid cell by −50 instead of 0.01 (diff(c(0, x)) starts from 0, not from the grid), which inflates its normalised density by 0.07% and puts its printed coverage off the exact value in 19 of its 300 frames, against 3 from the grid alone. The sphere script's normalising constant uses gamma(), which overflows from k = 344, so its density there is NaN; this page uses log-gamma.

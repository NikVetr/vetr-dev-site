Statistical intuitions

Small interactive figures, each isolating one statistical idea. Move a few parameters and watch the figure respond. Everything is computed exactly in your browser, by the same code a script can call through the API.

The geometry of correlation

Every variable is an arrow and every correlation the cosine of an angle. Rotate the arrows in 3-D to see why a correlation matrix must be positive semidefinite, which correlations can go together, how far apart k variables can be, and how much room one entry has.

Where is the probability mass?

In many dimensions a normal's density peaks at the mode, but its mass lies in a thin shell far from it, because volume grows with the distance. Watch density × volume as the dimension rises, see a mode and a mean mislead, and the points of a sphere crowd its equator.

Adjusting for the right variables

Confounders, mediators, colliders, M-bias and the front door, in linear causal graphs with numbers you set. See exactly what each adjustment set makes of the effect, which paths it opens or blocks, and how many weak colliders couple two independent variables.

Ways to break a p-value

When nothing is going on, a valid test's p-values are spread evenly from 0 to 1. Seven common shortcuts bend them: regressing out a batch first, removing covariates one at a time, a t-test on bootstrap means, correlating autocorrelated series, two-step filtering, ignoring pairing, and dropping pathways with no hits. See the false-positive rate each one really has.

How many principal components are noise?

The eigenvalues of pure noise spread over the Marchenko–Pastur law, so a large one is not by itself a signal. See the law for any number of observations and variables, when a real signal leaves the bulk, and why a small batch effect can take over the first principal component.

Traits evolving on a tree

Watch a trait evolve down the branches of a phylogeny under Brownian motion, a trend, a pull toward an optimum, an early or late burst, or a clade that shifts to an optimum of its own. Follow two traits through time in 3-D, and fill in a branch between fixed ends.

A tree is a covariance matrix

Under Brownian motion two species covary by the history they share, so a tree is a covariance matrix and its tips are arrows. Re-root it and the restricted likelihood stays put; step through Felsenstein's pruning, one cherry at a time, to the same number.

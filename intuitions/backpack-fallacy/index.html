The backpack fallacy

Two studies of the same kind report the same p-value, but one measured 2 people in each group and the other 16. Which of them is more likely to have found the larger true effect? It is tempting to say the small one: to reach the same p-value through so much noise, its effect must be strong, as a runner who keeps pace while carrying a backpack must be fast. Loken and Gelman called this reasoning the backpack fallacy. Whether it is a fallacy depends on how large true effects tend to be, and this page computes the answer exactly.

1 At the same p-value

Each study compares two groups of n people with a two-sample t-test. Every group's true mean is drawn at random, so every study has its own true difference between its groups, and its people vary around their group's mean with SD 1. 2 The true effects behind one p-value

The same p-value means different things for different sizes. A study with few people needs a large t to reach it, and its estimate is noisy, so the p-value fits a wide range of true differences, from small ones with lucky noise to large ones. A big study reaches it with a t near the normal's, and its true difference is pinned down. Whether the small study's range sits above the big study's depends on how often large true differences occur at all.

How it works

The model

As in backpack_fallacy.R, each of a study's two groups has a true mean drawn at random, from a Cauchy with scale τ or a normal with SD τ, and its n people vary around it with SD 1. The study's true difference Δ is then Cauchy with scale 2τ, or normal with SD √2 τ. Its two-sample t-test has 2n − 2 degrees of freedom, and given Δ its t is noncentral t with noncentrality Δ/√(2/n). A p-value fixes |t|, so it gives each study a distribution for |Δ|: the chance of that |t| under each Δ, weighted by how common that Δ is. Two studies are independent, and the chance that the smaller one's |Δ| is the larger follows from their two distributions.

Computed, not simulated

The script simulates ten million tests of each size, sorts them all by p-value, pairs each test with its neighbour, and in a sliding window of −log10 p reports the share of pairs in which the smaller study has the larger true difference. This page computes the same quantities by numerical integration instead: Simpson's rule on cells placed where each distribution lies, with the noncentral t's integral tabulated once for each size. Its numbers are accurate to about 10−5, and an update takes a fraction of a second, where the script takes minutes and needs ten gigabytes. The script's sliding average is computed exactly too, weighting each p-value by how often a pair of those two sizes meets there. Scripts and agents can get every number here through the API.

Checked against R

Rerun with ten million draws (set.seed(1)), the script's window averages agree with this page's in all 844 windows that hold at least 30 pairs, within their Monte Carlo error (none differs by more than 3 standard errors). The distributions of |Δ| agree with R's own integrals, by integrate() over the t statistic's mixture, to 10−6 in their quartiles, and the chances of a larger effect to 10−6.

What the script does differently

Its two-sided p-value is computed as log1p(exp(l)) + l from the one-sided log p-value l, which is log(p₁(1 + p₁)) rather than log(2p₁), so its axis is about 0.3 too far right for small p-values (the order of the tests, and so the pairing, is unaffected). This page uses the two-sided p-value. The script averages over ±1 of −log10 p, which near p = 1 pools p-values up to 0.1, so its curves start higher than the exact ones; the dashed lines show its average. It plots the log-odds as well as the chance; this page shows the chance. And it uses the same true means for every size, which does not change the answer, since each size's tests are paired at random.

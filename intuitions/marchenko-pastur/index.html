How many principal components are noise?

Principal components analysis ranks directions by the variance they carry, and the first few components are often read as the structure in the data. But pure noise, with every direction carrying the same variance σ², still gives sample eigenvalues that spread over a range. For n observations of p variables they follow the Marchenko–Pastur law, between σ²(1 − √γ)² and σ²(1 + √γ)², with γ = p/(n − 1). An eigenvalue inside that range is no evidence of anything. The panels show the law, when a real signal escapes it, and what the first two components show when the signal is a batch effect.

1 The eigenvalues of pure noise

Draw n observations of p independent variables, each with variance σ², and take the eigenvalues of their sample covariance. Every population eigenvalue is σ², but the sample ones spread out: the largest comes out too large and the smallest too small, the more so as p approaches n. As n and p grow together, their histogram settles on the Marchenko–Pastur density. Taken by rank, each eigenvalue has its own place in that spread, and the largest wanders either side of the upper edge.

2 When a signal leaves the bulk

Now give the population a few directions with extra variance, spikes: population eigenvalues 1 + θ against the noise's 1. A spike shows as a sample eigenvalue beyond the upper edge only when θ exceeds √γ, the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché threshold, and then it comes out too large, near (1 + θ)(1 + γ/θ). Below the threshold the spike is lost in the bulk, and the sample eigenvector that should point along it points nowhere in particular. Above it, the squared cosine between the two is (1 − γ/θ²)/(1 + γ/θ), short of 1 however clear the spike.

0 for no spike. The noise has variance 1 in every direction.

3 What the first two components show

The original script measures 200 samples on 500 features. Each feature is shaped by 49 latent factors and noise, and a batch, half the samples, shifts every feature by a small random amount: variance 10 against the 89 or so each feature has in all. Because the shift is shared across all 500 features, it adds up to a spike far above the factors', and the first principal component separates the batches cleanly. Regressing each feature on the batch removes the shift from every component exactly. With interactions, each factor's effect also differs between the batches, which that correction leaves in place.

How it works

The law

The sample covariance of n observations divides by n − 1 because the data are centred first, which uses up one degree of freedom, so the law's ratio is γ = p/(n − 1). Its density, √((b − x)(x − a)) / (2π σ² γ x) between the edges a and b, has a closed-form distribution function, which places each eigenvalue's expected position. When p exceeds n − 1 the covariance has rank n − 1, and the other p − n + 1 eigenvalues are exactly 0. The law describes the limit as n and p grow together; at finite sizes the largest eigenvalue fluctuates around the upper edge on a scale of n−2/3 (the Tracy–Widom law), so it falls outside the edge in a fair share of noise-only samples.

Computing

Each sample is drawn from shared/rng.js's mulberry32, normals by inversion, so a seed gives the same eigenvalues in every browser and in Node. The covariance's eigenvalues come from whichever of XᵀX and XXᵀ is smaller, reduced to tridiagonal form by Householder reflections and solved by the implicit QL method, in a background worker so the page stays responsive. A spike lies along a coordinate axis; since the noise looks the same in every direction, that changes no eigenvalue. Scripts and agents can run every panel through the API.

Spikes

The threshold and the two limits are those of Baik, Ben Arous and Péché (2005) and Paul (2007). They hold as n and p grow; at finite sizes a spike just past the threshold is lower than its limit and its eigenvector less aligned, and one just below it still lifts the largest eigenvalue a little.

The R scripts

marchenko–pastur_intuitions.R compares the simulated eigenvalues with RMTstat's law at ndf = n, the number of observations, but cov() centres the data, which leaves n − 1 degrees of freedom; at its 200 observations of 20 variables that puts its edges and its decision threshold, σ²(1 + √(p/n))², 0.12% low. This page uses n − 1. RMTstat's distribution function and quantiles are numerical and accurate to about 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻⁴; this page's are exact. The script's follow-up on random orthonormal matrices and rotations, which is about building correlation matrices from eigenvalues, is not part of this page.

1st_two_pc_axes.R corrects for the batch by regressing each feature on all 99 simulated variables, which only a simulation knows, and subtracting the batch's term, which leaves the batch means slightly unequal on every component. Here each feature is regressed on the batch alone, as one could with real data, which makes them equal exactly. Its comparison of discriminant analysis and support vector machines on held-out samples is not part of this page. Across 12 seeds of the script, PC 1 separates the batches by 1.72 ± 0.06 SDs and 65 eigenvalues lie above the noise edge; this page's version of its model gives the same.

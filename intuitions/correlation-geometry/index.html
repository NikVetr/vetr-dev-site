The geometry of correlation

Centre a variable's observations and scale them to length 1, and the variable becomes an arrow. The correlation of two variables is the cosine of the angle between their arrows. So a table of correlations is a table of angles, and it is a valid correlation matrix (positive semidefinite) exactly when some set of arrows has those angles. Each panel below turns one consequence of that into something you can rotate and push against.

1 A variable is an arrow

Take n observations of two variables, A and B. Subtract each variable's mean and divide what is left by its length: each variable is now an arrow of length 1 in n-dimensional observation space, one axis per observation. The dot product of the two arrows is Pearson's r, so r = cos θ for the angle θ between them. Centring puts every arrow on the plane where the coordinates sum to 0. With three observations that plane cuts the sphere in a great circle; with two, only two arrows are left.

2 Fixing a correlation draws a cone

Give a third variable, C, correlation rBC with B. C's arrow must be at angle cos⁻¹ rBC from B's, so it lies on a cone around B. With three observations C must also lie on the great circle, and the cone meets the circle at only two points, C₁ and C₂: B turned by that angle one way or the other. Any other variable A then has one of exactly two correlations with C. Each extra observation adds a dimension, so with four the two points become a circle of choices.

3 The cosine rule: which rAC are possible?

Now let the arrows use as many dimensions as they need. Fix rAB = cos α and rBC = cos β. C can sit anywhere on the cone of angle β around B. As it goes round, it comes closest to A on A's side of B, at angle |α − β|, and is farthest on the opposite side, at α + β. So rAC lies between cos(α + β) and cos(α − β), which is rABrBC ± √((1 − rAB²)(1 − rBC²)). The bound comes from adding angles, not correlations.

°

0° puts C on A's side of B (largest rAC), 180° opposite (smallest).

det R = 1 − rAB² − rBC² − rAC² + 2rABrBCrAC as rAC varies: the squared volume of the box the arrows span. It is 0 at both ends of the bar, largest at its centre rABrBC (◇), and negative outside, where no arrows exist. The dot is C's current value.

4 How far apart can k arrows be?

Two arrows can point opposite ways, r = −1. Three can be at most 120° apart in pairs (r = −½), four at most 109.5° (r = −⅓, the corners of a tetrahedron). In general k arrows can all have correlation r only if r ≥ −1/(k − 1). At that limit they are the vertices of a regular simplex centred on the origin, and they add up to zero. Agreement is easy; mutual disagreement is not.

5 One entry's room to move

Fix every entry of a correlation matrix but one. The free entry, rij, can still move within an interval: its centre is riᵀS⁻¹rj and its half-width √((1 − riᵀS⁻¹ri)(1 − rjᵀS⁻¹rj)), where S holds the correlations among the other variables and ri, rj their correlations with i and j. The half-width is the product of the parts of the two arrows that the others leave unexplained, and the centre is the value that makes det R largest. Choose a cell to see its interval; edit any entry to move it.

Expected room as variables are added

    Expected width of one entry's interval in a p × p matrix, both scales logarithmic. Drawn uniformly from all correlation matrices (LKJ with η = 1), the room shrinks steadily as the other variables explain more of each arrow. The sample correlation matrix of n observations of independent variables shrinks faster: its arrows live in n − 1 dimensions, so it is singular once p ≥ n. At p = n every entry sits at one end of its interval, and from p = n + 1 every interval has width 0: each entry is fixed by the rest. The diamond is the matrix above (its mean width over all cells).

    How it works

    Why the arrows exist

    Centring a variable's n values gives a vector whose entries sum to 0; dividing by its length gives a unit vector. Pearson's r is the sum of products of two such vectors, their dot product, so it is the cosine of the angle between them. A correlation matrix is the Gram matrix of its variables' arrows, which is why it must be positive semidefinite: xᵀRx is the squared length of a combination of arrows. Conversely, any positive semidefinite matrix with 1s on the diagonal is the Gram matrix of some unit arrows, given enough dimensions.

    Determinants are squared volumes

    det R is the squared volume of the parallelotope the arrows span (√det R is the volume). It is 1 when the arrows are at right angles and 0 when they are linearly dependent, which happens when the matrix sits on the boundary of the positive semidefinite set.

    Counting dimensions

    With n observations, the centred arrows live in n − 1 dimensions. A full-rank set of p arrows needs p ≤ n − 1, so a sample correlation matrix is singular whenever p ≥ n. With three observations the arrows have two dimensions to share, which is why panel 2's cone meets the circle in exactly two points; with n observations the arrows at a fixed angle to one arrow form a sphere of dimension n − 3.

    The intervals

    For one entry, write det R as det S × [(1 − a)(1 − b) − (rij − c)²], with a, b and c the quadratic forms in the panel 5 formula. The bracket is a downward parabola in rij, so the entry's interval is where it is non-negative. When the other variables are themselves linearly dependent the page uses the pseudo-inverse of S, which gives the same interval. The expected widths are exact: in a uniform matrix, the parts of i and j the others leave unexplained are independent products of independent Beta-distributed partial correlations (the C-vine of Lewandowski, Kurowicka and Joe, 2009); in a sample matrix each is Beta((n − p + 1)/2, (p − 2)/2). The API provides the same computations.
